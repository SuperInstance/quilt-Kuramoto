# 01 — ON THE TALLY-BOX AND ITS SIX VERBS

*Memoir the first of the Kaldfjord Circle. Read to the Circle, Thursday,
18 February 1904. Fair copy by M. Skavlan, checked against the author's draft by
H. Grønn and N. Krøger. The section "The Weigher" is Maren Skavlan's, from her
own manuscript, annexed to this memoir by her permission and mostly in her
words.*

**Halvard Grønn, pilot (retired)**

---

## §1. What a reckoner is

I went to sea at fifteen and took bearings for forty years, and I will say what
those years taught: a wrong assumption costs a hull, and a right one costs only
the trouble of writing it down. This memoir writes down what a reckoning is.

Let a **box** mean any honest worker whatsoever — a bookkeeper, a lightkeeper, a
pilot, a schoolmistress with her register, a cannery weighing fish. We hold that
every box, whatever else it may be, is five organs and nothing more:

1. **its settings** — the dials it can set and read (state);
2. **its judgment** — the rule by which it turns what is asked of it into a
   verdict (§4);
3. **its ledger** — the ordered record of its postings, every entry balanced
   (Memoir II);
4. **its clock** — the heartbeat that marks its watches (§5);
5. **its going-on** — the rule by which it proceeds from one heartbeat to the
   next.

A bearing is a judgment with a delay; a ledger is a judgment with a memory. The
five organs are what navigation, bookkeeping, and lightkeeping have in common
when they are done honestly.

## §2. The six verbs

Exactly six verbs touch the box. We have searched for a seventh for two winters
and found none that is not one of these six wearing another coat. (A conjecture,
this — see the close of §7.)

- **BIND.** To set a dial. Tolerances, thresholds, half-lives: settings are
  state, and only BIND writes them.
- **LINK.** To join two boxes, the egress of one to the ingress of another, so
  that one may ask of the other. A link forms only by consent of both ends and
  by name, never by resemblance: two wires that agree in shape but disagree in
  what they carry (fathoms against feet) must refuse to join.
- **EFFECT.** To post a balanced entry in the ledger and move the settings
  according to the going-on. One verb, one entry, one heartbeat's boundary.
- **VIEW.** To ask a linked box and be answered — within a bounded delay, from
  a state no older than a bounded staleness (Memoir III states the arithmetic).
- **TICK.** To sound the heartbeat. The tick is never starved by the traffic of
  questions; a heartbeat missed for the sake of answering is a fault, not an
  economy.
- **FORGET.** The sixth verb, and the one the Academy will like least. To close
  an account. **An account may be closed, never torn out; a receipt is always
  left.** A box whose receipt is missing has not forgotten; it has lied.

The six verbs are the whole grammar. Everything else the box does is one of
these six, or a habit of them.

## §3. The standing rules

We adopt seven standing rules. For each we write beside it what would break it,
for a rule that cannot be broken by a counterexample is no rule.

1. **Balance.** Every entry posted anywhere sums to nought, credit against
   debit. *Broken by a book-room that posts one side of an entry alone.* We do not
   prove this rule; we choose it, and everything in Memoir II is conditional on
   the choice.
2. **One clerk per account.** Each account belongs to exactly one box; only its
   owner posts to it. That is what an account *is*. *Broken by shared pens.*
3. **One pen, one book.** The events of a box are totally ordered by service:
   one matter at a time, and one matter is one posting's boundary. *Broken by a
   box with two simultaneous inner actors.*
4. **The counter-mark.** Every entry carries a unique mark; an entry whose mark
   is already in the book is passed over, not posted twice. *Broken by a clerk
   who posts the same invoice twice.* (Memoir II, §6 and the correspondence,
   record why "unique" must be *made* true — a mark built from the writer's name
   and his serial — and not merely assumed.)
5. **Bounded service.** Each verb completes within a counted number of
   heartbeats.
6. **The tick is never starved.** A pending heartbeat is served within a
   bounded window, whatever the traffic of questions. This is what keeps delay
   a property of the wiring and not of anyone's mercy.
7. **Views answer settled books.** A VIEW returns the state of a posted,
   settled entry; no half-written rows, and nothing written after the answer is
   sealed.

Rules 5–7 are used in Memoir III; rules 1–4 carry Memoir II.

## §4. The Weigher *(Maren Skavlan's section)*

A judgment is a set of **keyed answers** — name against name — matched against a
**tolerance dial**, and the dial is part of the box's settings. To question the
box is to hold a thing against the keyed answers and measure resemblance. The
measure we call a **gauge**: a rule d assigning to every two things a number,
never negative, zero of a thing with itself, returning the same number either
way round, and obeying the triangle rule — the direct way is never longer than
the way round by a third thing. [The reader will recognize the three demands of
a distance; the word *gauge* is the Circle's, the notion needs no other. — Ed.]
The verdict is:

- **WITHIN(k)** — exactly one key answers within the dial;
- **AMBIGUOUS** — several answer;
- **WITHOUT** — none answers.

**The law of the dial.** *Widening the dial can only widen acceptance: what was
answered within a narrow dial is answered within every wider one.* For if a key
answered within r, it answers within any r′ ≥ r. The proof is one line and we
give it in full because it is the model of the longer proofs to come: nothing
here is used but the meaning of the dial.

**Aliases are data.** Things at gauge-distance nought we call **aliases**. The
pink salmon and the humpy are one fish under two names; the alias class — all
names at distance nought from one another — is the fish's *true name*, and the
gauge descends to the alias classes without complaint (if two pairs of aliases
are measured once by one pair of representatives and once by another, the two
measures agree; this we verified, and the verification is three lines of the
triangle rule, printed in the appendix of the minute-book). No judge may
pretend aliased names are different fish, or that names are real if the dial,
turned down, would erase them. When the box says AMBIGUOUS it need not guess —
**it prints the set**. Let the fisherman decide; the weigher's honesty is that
it never decides for him.

**The doubling gauge.** For quantities that multiply rather than add — a count
of fish, a sum of money — the useful gauge is d(x, y) = |log x − log y| (the
logarithms are read from the tables). It obeys all three demands, since it is
the ordinary difference of numbers carried through the logarithm; and it is
*the* gauge in which "within a factor of two" is a ball: d(x, y) ≤ log 2 if and
only if x lies between y/2 and 2y. Every gate of the form "half to double" in
these annals is a dial of log 2 on this gauge.

**The two-clause lemma (stated; developed in Memoir III).** If a stage of
accuracy ρ stands before a judge of dial r, then: what is truly within r − ρ
is answered WITHIN; what is answered WITHIN is truly within r + ρ. Certainty
pays the stage's error in advance; soundness collects it afterwards. The proof
is the triangle rule, twice, and Memoir III builds the whole theory of drift
upon it.

## §5. On choosing the unit

Because the weigher compares in whole numbers, the unit is part of the judgment,
and a unit badly chosen is a verdict badly blurred. Two rules:

**The covering rule.** *Measure n quantities in a unit of b smallest steps, and
no true point of the world lies farther than b·√n / 2 from the nearest
representable point.* For rounding each quantity to its nearest step errs by
less than b/2 in each direction, and n such errors compounded by the square
upon the hypotenuse give √n · b/2; and the corner of the cell — every quantity
exactly half a step from its neighbors — attains the bound, so it is exact. To
hold a tolerance ε in n quantities it suffices that b ≤ 2ε/√n. Choose the
report so the constants come out whole: the 3–4–5 triangle and its kin put the
wanted quantity *on* the lattice, and the covering error is nought. (We are
indebted to M. de Minkowski's geometry of numbers for the company; our lemma is
elementary and proved above.)

**The squared comparison.** To judge "within Δ" as |g − s| ≤ Δ, compare instead
|g − s|² against Δ². Since the square is strictly increasing upon the
non-negative numbers, the two comparisons decide identically; the second needs
no root and no division, and two clerks — or two boxes at two benches keeping
same arithmetic — cannot disagree about the verdict. The weakest pen in the
room sets the arithmetic for everyone.

## §6. Boxes in a web

Boxes LINK into a **web** (we say: the boxes and their links make a *cell
graph*). Composition of boxes is wiring and nothing else: no middleman reorders,
no station buffers by its own policy, no interpreter stands between. What
travels on a link is a flit: {verb, from, to, three arguments, datum}. The
web's correctness is the wiring's correctness; where a wire is wrongly made,
the fault is in the wire, and Memoir II's localization rule (due to Vik) finds
it by walking from the wrong number to the sensor, through every cell in
between: a wrong displayed value is a wrong cell, a wrong wire, or a wrong raw
reading, and there is no fourth place for error to hide.

## §7. What this memoir claims, and what it does not

It claims: that five organs and six verbs exhaust the honest worker; that
judgment is matching-with-tolerance with the dial as state; that aliases are
data; that the unit is part of the verdict; that composition is wiring. It does
not claim: that the six verbs are the only *possible* grammar (we conjecture it
and register the counterexample that would kill us: a seventh verb that is not
a habit of the six, load-bearing for some theorem the six cannot carry); that
any dial is right (the dial is the task's business, not ours); that the world
stands still (Memoirs III and IV are the annals of its not standing still).

The Circle's rule stands at the foot of every page: name what would falsify
each thing said. This page names its own: a box needing a seventh verb, a gauge
without the triangle rule that yet supports the two-clause lemma, a verdict
that must guess. We have looked. Bring us one.

## Appendix — what the first draft got wrong *(printed by order of the
meeting, 21 April 1904; the draft itself is kept among the drafts, faults
and all)*

Four faults, all caught by letter before the fair copy was made, all kept
on the record:

1. **The draft demanded separation** (distance-nought things the same
   thing). Skavlan's letter of 12 November 1903 killed it with the pink
   salmon and the humpy — two names, one fish — and §4 above is rebuilt on
   the ruin: the gauge without separation, the alias class as the fish's
   true name, aliases as data.
2. **The draft's dial was set once** ("at the making and needs no
   minding"). Struck in the same letter: a dial that cannot be turned is a
   painting. The dial is state (§1), and Memoirs III and IV are the annals
   of its needing to be turned.
3. **The draft's Box guessed** ("choose the nearest and return it"). A
   weigher that guesses is a liar with a dial; the verdicts of §4 print
   the set and never choose.
4. **The draft had five verbs**, and closed accounts by "closed and
   struck." The sixth verb — FORGET, the receipt always left — entered
   the school's grammar by Skavlan's demand in the same letter, from her
   school registers: a child who leaves is struck off; a fee that is
   closed carries a receipt.

And one debt the draft owed and this revision pays: the two-clause lemma
was called "obvious and not proved here." Krøger's letter of 2 December
1903 asked for the proof anyway — on the water, the obvious bearings are
the ones that put you on the rocks — and §4 now gives it. The school's
rule dates from that exchange: **a claim marked obvious is a claim marked
unexamined.**
