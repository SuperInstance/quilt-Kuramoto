# 00 — THE EDITOR'S PREFACE

**Annals of the Kaldfjord Circle, vol. I (1903–1905), with the drafts and
the second generation**

These annals came to light in March of this year, in the loft of the
Strandsild cannery at Kaldfjord, Finnmark, during the survey that preceded
the building's demolition. They were in a steel trunk of the kind the
telegraph service used for instruments. The trunk held more than we first
said, and this edition prints all of it: five fair-copied memoirs, **the
five first drafts they were corrected from** — over-claims, conflations,
one unbalanced entry and one "plainly" that was not — a **printed slip**
pasted into every copy of the second memoir, a minute-book of Thursday
meetings, twenty-six letters sorted by writer, a comptometer ribbon spooled
on a wooden dowel, and, loose at the bottom, a **1923 offprint by a pupil
of the school, with four letters folded into it.** The trunk's seal was
intact. The cannery's last manager, it appears, simply closed the loft in
1908 and let the building hold the school's papers for it — and someone
afterwards added the 1923 packet to the same keeping.

We publish as found, and we publish the *development*, not only the
results. The school's own doctrine — made a rule of the house on 4 May
1904, the day the slip was countersigned — was that the drafts are kept,
the corrections are printed, and nothing is torn out. An edition that
printed only the fair copies would flatter the school in exactly the way
the school forbade itself. So: the first drafts are printed in the drafts
folder, faults and all; the fair copies carry appendices naming what the
drafts got wrong and who caught it; the correspondence is printed whole,
including the letters that stung; and the second generation's paper is
printed with the founders' answers to it, because the trunk kept those
too.

The order of the volume: the memoirs in the order the minute-book gives
them (the tally-box, February 1904; the balance of the books, May 1904;
the parallax memoirs, October 1904; the bell-rope, February 1905, refused
at Kristiania in June; the ledger papers, July–August 1905); then the
correspondence in two parts (the making, the closing); then the second
generation; then the editors' colophon, which maps every fault and every
repair to what the reader will make of them.

The transcription policy is minimal and is stated once, here. Their
notation is kept where it is unambiguous (they write ρ for a rate of
drift, F for a staleness in days, the signs + and − for credit and debit;
they count money in kroner and øre, fish by the barrel, distance in
fathoms, time in days and four-hour watches). Where the school used a word
of their own coinage — *box*, *gauge*, *fold*, *cut* — the word is kept
and defined at first use, in their definition, not ours. Square brackets
mark the editors' additions; everything else is theirs, including the
errors, which are the point of this edition.

One thing the reader should be told before going further, because the
Circle would have insisted on it: they kept no calculating machine larger
than Johanne Fosse's comptometer, and they trusted no figure that had not
been carried through three times by two different hands. Every table was
so verified; where the passes disagreed, the disagreement is printed.
Halvard Grønn's rule, from the minute-book of 14 January 1904, stands as
the school's epigraph and might as well stand as ours:

> A rule that cannot be broken by a counterexample is no rule. A proof
> that hides its corrections is a false proof.

What follows is village mathematics in the strict sense: done at night, by
people who worked all day, on the only subject they all shared — the
keeping of honest accounts in a world that moves. The Circle believed
their subject was general, and they corrected one another the way people
correct family: promptly, in writing, and forever. The reader will decide
whether they were right; we note only that the trunk was sealed, the books
balanced, the bell was rung on the hour, and the drafts were kept.

— The Editors
